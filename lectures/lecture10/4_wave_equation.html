Numerical Solution of the Wave Equation

Numerical Solution of the Wave Equation

The wave equation is one of the most fundamental equations in physics, describing how disturbances propagate through space and time. Unlike the diffusion equation which smooths out concentrations, the wave equation preserves and propagates energy in the form of traveling waves.

This document will guide you through: - Understanding the physics of wave propagation - Discretizing the wave equation numerically - Implementing an explicit finite difference solver - Analyzing wave behavior and stability - Exploring different boundary conditions and initial conditions

Physical Model

Understanding Wave Propagation

The wave equation describes how disturbances propagate without dissipation. Think of: - Sound waves in air - Electromagnetic waves in vacuum - Vibrations on a guitar string - Water waves on a pond surface

Unlike diffusion (where particles spread randomly), waves carry energy and information at a definite speed while maintaining their shape.

The Wave Equation

The one-dimensional wave equation is:

\[\frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial t^2} = c^2 \frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial x^2}\]

where: - \(u(x,t)\) is the wave amplitude (displacement, pressure, electric field, etc.) - \(c\) is the wave speed (constant for the medium) - The equation is second-order in both space and time

Key differences from diffusion equation: - Second-order in time: Requires both initial position AND velocity - Hyperbolic PDE: Waves propagate at finite speed - Energy conservation: No dissipation (in the ideal case) - Reversible: Waves can bounce back

Physical Interpretation

The wave equation tells us that:

  1. Acceleration \(\frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial t^2}\) is proportional to curvature \(\frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial x^2}\)
  2. Regions with positive curvature accelerate upward
  3. Regions with negative curvature accelerate downward
  4. This creates propagating waves at speed \(c\)

From Continuous to Discrete

Spatial Discretization

Just like with diffusion, we discretize space into a grid:

\[x_i = i \Delta x, \quad i = 0, 1, 2, \ldots, N\]

The spatial second derivative uses the same finite difference formula:

\[\frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial x^2}\bigg|_{x_i} \approx \frac{u_{i+1} - 2u_i + u_{i-1}}{(\Delta x)^2}\]

Temporal Discretization: Central Differences

This is where waves differ from diffusion! The second-order time derivative requires a different approach:

\[\frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial t^2}\bigg|_t^n \approx \frac{u^{n+1} - 2u^n + u^{n-1}}{(\Delta t)^2}\]

This gives us the explicit update formula:

\[u_i^{n+1} = 2u_i^n - u_i^{n-1} + \frac{c^2 (\Delta t)^2}{(\Delta x)^2} (u_{i+1}^n - 2u_i^n + u_{i-1}^n)\]

Matrix Representation

We can write this in matrix form:

\[\mathbf{u}^{n+1} = 2\mathbf{u}^n - \mathbf{u}^{n-1} + r^2 \mathbf{M} \mathbf{u}^n\]

where:

  • \(r = \frac{c \Delta t}{\Delta x}\) is the Courant number
  • \(\mathbf{M}\) is the same second-difference matrix as before
Dirichlet Boundary Conditions (Fixed Values)

For fixed boundaries (like a guitar string clamped at both ends), the matrix \(\mathbf{M}\) is:

\[\mathbf{M} = \begin{bmatrix} -2 & 1 & 0 & 0 & \cdots & 0\\ 1 & -2 & 1 & 0 & \cdots & 0\\ 0 & 1 & -2 & 1 & \cdots & 0\\ \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \ddots & \ddots & \vdots\\ 0 & \cdots & 0 & 1 & -2 & 1\\ 0 & \cdots & 0 & 0 & 1 & -2\\ \end{bmatrix}\]

Physical example: Guitar string fixed at both ends, electromagnetic waves in a cavity.

Neumann Boundary Conditions (Free Ends)

For free boundaries (zero slope: \(\frac{\partial u}{\partial x} = 0\)), the matrix becomes:

\[\mathbf{M}_{\text{Neumann}} = \begin{bmatrix} -1 & 1 & 0 & 0 & \cdots & 0\\ 1 & -2 & 1 & 0 & \cdots & 0\\ 0 & 1 & -2 & 1 & \cdots & 0\\ \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \ddots & \ddots & \vdots\\ 0 & \cdots & 0 & 1 & -2 & 1\\ 0 & \cdots & 0 & 0 & 1 & -1\\ \end{bmatrix}\]

Physical example: Sound waves in a tube with open ends, free vibration of a rod.

The CFL Stability Condition

This is crucial for wave equations! The explicit scheme is only stable if:

\[\text{CFL} = \frac{c \Delta t}{\Delta x} \leq 1\]

CFL Condition: The Stability Requirement

The Courant-Friedrichs-Lewy (CFL) condition ensures numerical stability:

\[\frac{c \Delta t}{\Delta x} \leq 1\]

Physical interpretation: The numerical wave speed must not exceed the physical wave speed.

What happens if violated:

  • \(\text{CFL} > 1\): Solution becomes unstable and blows up
  • \(\text{CFL} = 1\): Perfect transmission, no numerical dispersion
  • \(\text{CFL} < 1\): Stable but introduces numerical dispersion

Practical choice: Use \(\text{CFL} \approx 0.8\) for good stability margin.
